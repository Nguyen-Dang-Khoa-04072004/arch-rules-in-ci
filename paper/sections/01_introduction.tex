\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}

Architecture-as-Code (AaC) formalizes architectural intent as executable specifications that can be checked automatically within development workflows and CI pipelines. In practice, tools such as ArchUnit for the JVM, import-linter for Python, and dependency-cruiser for JavaScript and TypeScript enable teams to codify layering, dependency, cycle, and visibility constraints as part of their delivery process. The promise is compelling: architecture rules can provide continuous feedback, reduce divergence between intended and implemented structures, and support governance in fast-changing ecosystems. Yet there remains a striking lack of large-scale, longitudinal evidence on how these rules are adopted, what they encode, how they evolve over time, and whether they causally improve architectural outcomes in real-world repositories.

This paper addresses that gap. We study a large sample of non-template GitHub repositories across the JVM, Python, and JavaScript/TypeScript ecosystems, mining executable architecture rules at scale and reconstructing their historical evolution. Our analysis is grounded in a canonical rule schema, continuous historical snapshots, and a staggered difference-in-differences design. The core research problem is not whether architecture rules can work in theory, but whether they are actually used, whether they remain operational in the face of refactoring and exceptions, and whether their deployment translates into measurable structural improvements.

Our study is motivated by three tensions in current AaC research. First, most evidence focuses on a few example systems or handcrafted benchmarks rather than the long tail of real-world repositories. Second, the field often treats architecture rules as stable artifacts, although in practice they are continuously edited, exempted, relaxed, and sometimes abandoned. Third, causal evidence remains scarce: even when rules are present, it is unclear whether adoption genuinely reduces violations or merely coincides with other engineering efforts. This paper interrogates these assumptions directly.

The contributions are fourfold. C1: We construct a large mining dataset of architecture rule files from multiple ecosystems and publish it as \ArchRules, alongside scripts for canonical parsing and historical replay. C2: We define the construct of \rrot, characterizing the gradual decay and exemption-driven weakening of executable rules over time, and map these patterns to a taxonomy of rule categories. C3: We evaluate the causal effect of rule adoption using a Callaway-Sant'Anna staggered DiD design with matching, producing ATT estimates across event time and heterogeneous treatment effects. C4: We provide a benchmark and empirical evidence base for researchers and practitioners developing future AaC, governance, and CONTAaC frameworks.
