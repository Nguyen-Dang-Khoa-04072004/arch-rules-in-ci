\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We analyzed data collected from 4,300 repositories, with a total of 4,321 tool-related observations, including 11,098 rule files and 86,339 recorded edits.
Research findings are organized by research question. For each question, we first present the main results and figures. Next, we examine the differences between tools. Finally, we assess whether the findings support the corresponding research hypothesis.

\subsection{RQ1: To what extent do repositories expose evidence of architecture-checker invocation in GitHub Actions workflows?}
\label{sec:rq1}

To answer RQ1, we examined the default-branch GitHub Actions
workflows of repositories using import-linter and
dependency-cruiser. We hypothesized that fewer than half of the
repositories using each tool would invoke it in CI.
Table~\ref{tab:ci_results} and Figure~\ref{fig:ci_share} show
the proportions of repository-tool observations meeting this
criterion.

For import-linter, we found that 351 out of 826 observations satisfy
the integration criterion (42.5\%, 95\% CI: 39.4-45.8\%). For
dependency-cruiser, we found that only 125 out of 1,073 observations
meet the criterion (11.6\%, 95\% CI: 9.8-13.5\%). Both proportions
are below 50\%, which supports our hypothesis for these two tools.
We also combined the observations from both tools and found that
476 out of 1,899 observations satisfy the criterion (25.1\%, 95\%
CI: 23.1--27.1\%). This result is also below the hypothesized
threshold of 50\%.

In ArchUnit observations, 869 out of 2,421 with an available workflow result contain at least one
of these commands (35.9\%, 95\% CI: 34.1-37.9\%). Because ArchUnit
does not provide a dedicated CI command, this result only shows that
the repository invokes a potentially relevant Maven or Gradle build
or test task. It does not confirm that ArchUnit rules are actually
executed. Therefore, we do not use this result to evaluate the hypothesis.

The observed workflow-invocation rates differ across the two tools
with dedicated commands: import-linter's rate is approximately
3.7 times that of dependency-cruiser. This contrast should be
interpreted in light of the tool-specific detection criteria rather
than as a direct comparison of equivalent enforcement mechanisms.
In particular, the ArchUnit estimate is a build-or-test proxy and
is not directly comparable to the dedicated-command measures.

\subsection{RQ2: Which architecture constraint dominates executable rules, and how do they differ across tools?}
\label{sec:rq2}
To answer RQ2, we classified 21,713 held-out constructs using our
predefined taxonomy, which applies the same categories to all three
tools (Table~\ref{tab:rule_taxonomy}). Our parser successfully
categorized 15,173 of 17,907 ArchUnit constructs (84.7\%),
2,136 of 2,265 import-linter constructs (94.3\%), and
1,503 of 1,541 dependency-cruiser constructs (97.5\%). The remaining
2,901 constructs were not categorized and were excluded from the
category proportions. Figure~\ref{fig:categories_by_tool} shows
the distribution of the categorized constructs for each tool.


We found that dependency and layering constraints account for
98.1\% of categorized import-linter constructs (95\% CI:
97.5--98.6\%). For ArchUnit and dependency-cruiser, these
constraints account for 58.5\% (95\% CI: 57.8-59.3\%) and
56.8\% (95\% CI: 54.4-59.1\%), respectively. Only the
import-linter confidence interval lies entirely above 70\%.
Therefore, our results support the hypothesis for import-linter,
but not for ArchUnit or dependency-cruiser.

We also found differences in the other constraint categories.
For dependency-cruiser, cycle constraints account for 649 of
1,503 categorized constructs (43.2\%). For ArchUnit, naming and
annotation constraints account for 18.5\% and 8.4\%,
respectively. Visibility and other constraints together account
for an additional 11.5\%. We further compared the overall category
distributions and found a difference across the three tools
($\chi^2 = 5865.56$, $df = 12$, Cram\'er's $V = 0.395$).

These results show that the three tools have different constraint
profiles. Import-linter focuses almost entirely on dependency and
layering constraints. Dependency-cruiser also covers these
constraints, but a substantial proportion of its constructs focus
on cycle constraints. In contrast, ArchUnit covers a wider range
of constraint types, including naming and annotation rules.
Therefore, the lower proportion of dependency and layering
constraints in ArchUnit does not necessarily mean that these
constraints are less important in its rules. Instead, it reflects
a more diverse distribution of constraint categories.

\input{figures/fig3_categories_by_tool}

\begin{keyfinding}
Our results show that dependency and layering constraints
dominate import-linter (98.1\%), but account for smaller shares
of ArchUnit (58.5\%) and dependency-cruiser (56.8\%). Therefore,
our hypothesis is supported only for import-linter. We also
found that the distribution of constraint categories differs
across the three tools (Cram\'er's $V = 0.395$).
\end{keyfinding}


\subsection{RQ3: How often are architecture rules exempted, disabled, or left unchanged?}
\label{sec:rq3}

To answer RQ3, we analyzed the edit histories of rule files to
determine how often architecture rules are exempted or disabled.
We defined rule rot as the first edit that exempts or disables
a rule in its file history (Section~\ref{sec:background}).
We then estimated the probability of this event occurring
within 24 months using the Kaplan-Meier estimator.

Across all tools, we found that the estimated 24-month incidence
of rule rot is 26.3\% (95\% CI: 25.4-27.2\%). This proportion
is below the hypothesized threshold of 30\%. However, the
results differ considerably across tools. ArchUnit has the
lowest estimated incidence at 12.9\% (8,579 files; 95\% CI:
12.1-13.8\%). In contrast, the estimated incidence is 58.1\%
for import-linter (1,093 files; 95\% CI: 54.0-62.3\%) and
80.5\% for dependency-cruiser (1,426 files; 95\% CI:
78.4-82.7\%). These results show that the overall estimate
hides substantial differences between the three tools.
While ArchUnit remains below the 30\% threshold, both
import-linter and dependency-cruiser exceed it.

We also examined how many rule files remain unchanged after
their introduction. We found that 5,501 out of 11,098 files
(49.6\%, 95\% CI: 48.6-50.4\%) receive no subsequent
modifications. This proportion varies across tools: 55.7\%
for ArchUnit, 13.9\% for import-linter, and 40.2\% for
dependency-cruiser. However, this measure is different from
the rule-rot incidence reported above. A file is considered
unchanged only when it has no subsequent edits, whereas rule
rot occurs when an edit specifically exempts or disables a
rule. Therefore, a file can be modified without experiencing
rule rot.

Among the 5,122 files with at least 24 months of follow-up,
we found that 49.3\% remain unchanged (95\% CI:
48.0-50.7\%). We report this result separately because it
only includes files with sufficient follow-up time. In
contrast, our Kaplan--Meier analysis accounts for files
with incomplete follow-up through right-censoring. Thus,
these two estimates describe related but different aspects
of rule-file maintenance.

We further analyzed the types of edits recorded in our
dataset. Table~\ref{tab:edit_class_counts} presents the
distribution of the automatically assigned edit classes.
We found that restructure is the most common edit type,
accounting for 69,047 out of 86,339 edits (80.0\%).
Exemption and disabling edits together account for only
4.7\% of all recorded edits. Although these edits represent
a relatively small proportion of all changes, their first
occurrence defines the rule-rot event in our primary
Kaplan--Meier analysis.

As a supplementary analysis, we also examined a Cox
proportional hazards model. The model reports a hazard ratio
of 1.307 (95\% CI: 1.270-1.345) for the log file-level
commit count and 0.850 (95\% CI: 0.793-0.911) for the
log author count. However, this model examines the time to
the first loosening or exemption edit, which covers a
broader range of events than the exemption or disabling
definition used in our primary analysis. Its estimated
24-month incidence is 41.9\% (95\% CI: 40.9-43.0\%).
Therefore, we treat this model as a supplementary analysis
of a broader maintenance event rather than a direct
estimate of our primary rule-rot outcome.

Overall, our results do not support the hypothesis that
the 24-month incidence of rule rot exceeds the 30\%
threshold across the entire dataset. However, the
tool-specific results reveal substantial differences
between ecosystems. We also found that nearly half of
the rule files receive no subsequent modifications.
Nevertheless, the absence of modifications does not
necessarily indicate the absence of rule rot, just as
the presence of modifications does not necessarily
indicate rule rot. Most recorded edits are classified
as restructure, and our automatic edit classification
has not been validated against manually annotated
examples. Therefore, we interpret these results
with this limitation in mind.

\subsection{RQ4: How do dependency and layering violations change around the adoption of ArchUnit rules?}
\label{sec:rq4}

To answer RQ4, we examined how dependency and layering violations
change around the adoption of ArchUnit rules. We defined adoption
as the first commit that introduces a parseable ArchUnit rule.
Our initial comparison set contains 2,422 repository--tool
observations. Among these, we identified 317 repositories with
at least 12 months of observable history both before and after
the adoption commit.

We used these 317 repositories to create two separate analysis
sets (Table~\ref{tab:rq4_cohort}). For the ratifier and reformer
classification, we excluded 19 repositories that did not have
a violation count either one month before adoption or at the
adoption point. These missing values occurred because no commit
was available at the required observation point or because the
import-graph replay failed. This left us with 298 repositories
for the classification analysis.

For the violation trajectory analysis, we used a separate
selection process. We excluded 169 repositories that did not
have any dependency or layering rule that our replay process
could translate. This left 148 repositories. We excluded these
repositories to avoid treating a zero violation count from an
untranslated rule as evidence of a clean architecture. Among
the 148 repositories, seven did not have complete violation
counts at all three observation points ($t{=}{-}1$, $t{=}0$,
and $t{=}12$). Therefore, we used the remaining 141 repositories
for the trajectory analysis.

\input{tables/table_rq4_cohort}

In this analysis, we focused only on dependency and layering
constraints. Across the evaluated observations, we found that
the mean coverage of this constraint family is 52.9\%, while
the mean evaluable coverage is 33.5\%. These two measures
describe different aspects of the replay process and use
different denominators. Therefore, we report and interpret
them separately.

A ratifier has $V(-1){=}0$. That predicate leaves $V(0)$
free. Of the 240, 157 have no translated dependency or
layering rule, so the zero is a sum over an empty check
list. Sixty-six have at least one evaluable rule,
$V(0){=}0$, and no observed pre-adoption violation.
Sixteen have at least one evaluable rule and $V(0){>}0$.
The remaining ratifier is also a reformer.
The ratifier name is therefore silent about compliance
at the adoption commit.

A reformer has an observed pre-adoption violation and
$V(0){=}0$. One reformer also has $V(-1){=}0$, so
$240 + 7$ counts that repository twice. The other six
reformers have $V(-1){>}0$.

The repositories outside both labels number
$298 - 240 - 7 + 1 = 52$ (17.4\%). Each has
$V(-1){>}0$ and $V(0){>}0$, and each has at least one
evaluable rule. They are not among the 19 repositories
removed for a missing snapshot. Both counts were observed.
They are a third group: violations were present one month
before adoption and were still present at the adoption
commit.
These results describe the state of the evaluated
constraints around the adoption of ArchUnit. However, they do
not directly measure the effect of adopting ArchUnit rules.

We further examined the violation trajectories of the 141
repositories with complete observations. Table~\ref{tab:rq4_trajectories}
and Figure~\ref{fig:rq4_change} present the results. We found
that the median violation count is zero at all three observation
points: one month before adoption, at adoption, and 12 months
after adoption. The interquartile range is 0--11 at each of
these points. In addition, 83 out of 141 repositories already
have zero evaluable violations one month before adoption.

The mean violation counts are 73.3 (95\% CI: 23.9-138.2)
before adoption, 85.6 (95\% CI: 28.6-154.8) at adoption,
and 103.9 (95\% CI: 33.8-196.2) 12 months after adoption.
When comparing the first and last observation points, we found
that violations decreased in 18 repositories (12.8\%, 95\% CI:
7.1-17.7\%), remained unchanged in 86 repositories (61.0\%,
95\% CI: 53.2-69.5\%), and increased in 37 repositories
(26.2\%, 95\% CI: 19.1-33.3\%).

We also used within-repository ordinary least squares regression
to estimate the violation trends before and after adoption.
The mean pre-adoption slope is 1.63 violations per month
(95\% CI: -0.09 to 4.38), while the mean post-adoption slope
is 1.83 violations per month (95\% CI: 0.47 to 3.74). These
results do not show a consistent decline in violations after
adoption. Instead, the mean violation counts increase across
the three observation points, and the estimated post-adoption
slope is also positive. The median remains zero and the
interquartile range remains 0--11, so the distribution is
right-skewed and the mean is driven by repositories with
relatively large violation counts.

Overall, the 298 repositories with counts at $t{=}{-}1$
and $t{=}0$ split into 240 ratifiers ($V(-1){=}0$), seven
reformers, and 52 repositories with violations at both
snapshots, with one repository in both of the first two
counts. Sixteen ratifiers have violations at adoption, and
157 ratifiers have no evaluable rule.
Among the 141 repositories with complete trajectories, most
show no change in violation counts between the first and last
observation points. We also found that more repositories
experience an increase in violations than a decrease. In
addition, 83 repositories already have zero evaluable violations
before adopting ArchUnit rules. These findings describe the
observed changes in repositories that adopted ArchUnit and met
our history and evaluability requirements. However, we cannot
conclude that ArchUnit adoption causes these changes because
our analysis does not include an untreated comparison group.
Therefore, our results should be interpreted as descriptive
patterns rather than estimates of the causal effect of
ArchUnit adoption.

Our results show that 240 of 298 repositories (80.5\%)
