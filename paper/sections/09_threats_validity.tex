\section{Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:threats}

\subsection{Construct Validity}
\label{sec:threats-construct}

Our CI measures capture evidence of architecture-checker invocation
from GitHub Actions workflow definitions rather than confirmed
execution. For import-linter and dependency-cruiser, tool-specific
command references indicate configured invocation. For ArchUnit,
we use Maven or Gradle build and test tasks as proxies for potential
invocation. These proxies do not establish that ArchUnit tests are
present or that architectural constraints are actually executed.
Therefore, the reported CI proportions should be interpreted as
evidence of configured or potentially relevant invocation, rather
than successful execution of architecture checks.

Our taxonomy uses a predefined mapping based on API names and
keywords, developed from an initial set of 1,000 tool-specific
repository entries and then applied to the remaining constructs.
The classification set contains 21,713 constructs, of which
18,812 are assigned to one of seven categories. The remaining
2,901 constructs are uncategorized and excluded from category
proportions. Although the fixed mapping provides a consistent
basis for comparing the three tools, we do not validate its
assignments against an independently annotated reference set.
Consequently, the reported category distributions reflect the
output of our automated classification procedure and may not
capture every architectural constraint correctly.

We operationalize rule rot at the rule-file level as the first
observed edit classified as either \emph{exempt} or \emph{disable}.
This operationalization relies on automated signals from file
changes and configuration patterns rather than verifying the
semantic effect of every edit on individual constraints. For
example, a newly introduced file containing a non-failing severity
setting may be classified as an exemption, while an occurrence
of an exception-related term in a comment may also trigger this
classification. Such cases may be detected as weakening events
even when they do not represent an actual reduction in enforcement.
We therefore interpret the identified events as candidate rule-rot
events rather than manually confirmed instances. The detected
timestamp identifies the first qualifying edit in the observed
history, but does not establish the author's intent or whether
the change was justified.

Our rule-history reconstruction also has limitations. We use the
earliest available commit in the reconstructed history as the
start of the observation period, which may not correspond to the
file's original creation. In addition, file renames may interrupt
the reconstructed history, and matching rules by construct names
may fail to detect the removal of one rule when another rule with
the same construct name remains. These limitations may affect
the identification and timing of the first detected event.
Furthermore, \emph{restructure} is a residual edit category that
includes both changes to rule files that do not alter parsed
constraints and other edits not assigned to the remaining
categories.

Finally, our ArchUnit replay evaluates only dependency and
layering constraints that can be translated into checks over
source-level import graphs. Other constraint types, including
cycle, naming, annotation, and visibility constraints, fall
outside the replay procedure. Since we analyze reconstructed
import graphs rather than executing ArchUnit tests, the resulting
violation counts represent only the constraints that our replay
procedure can evaluate.

\subsection{Internal Validity}
\label{sec:threats-internal}

Our CI proportions, category distributions, and rule-rot estimates
describe the workflows, constructs, and rule-file histories
observed in the study corpus. They do not establish the effect
of adopting an architecture checker. In particular, the estimated
rule-rot incidence depends on an automated event detector whose
classifications may include false positives and may not capture
every semantic weakening of a constraint.

For RQ3, we calculate confidence intervals for the
Kaplan-Meier estimates and the proportion of rule files
with no subsequent modifications using file-level
bootstrap resampling with 1,000 replicates. We resample
individual files independently, including files from the
same repository. Consequently, these intervals do not
account for potential within-repository dependence and
may underestimate uncertainty. The supplementary Cox
model uses conventional Wald confidence intervals without
repository-level clustering or a robust sandwich variance
estimator. In addition, its commit and author count
covariates are calculated over each file's entire observed
history and may therefore include activity occurring
after the event. We interpret the resulting hazard ratios
as exploratory associations rather than evidence that
these characteristics predict event occurrence.

Our ArchUnit analysis is also descriptive. Of the 2,422
tool-specific repository entries, 317 repositories meet the
requirement of at least 12 months of observable history before
and after the first parseable ArchUnit rule. We further restrict
the longitudinal analysis to 141 repositories that have at least
one evaluable dependency or layering constraint and complete
violation observations at one month before the introduction
commit, at the introduction commit, and 12 months afterward.
This selection excludes repositories without constraints that
our replay procedure can evaluate, as well as repositories with
incomplete observations. Therefore, the findings represent
repositories that meet these history and evaluability criteria
rather than all repositories containing ArchUnit rules.

Changes in violation counts may also reflect other development
activities during the observation period, rather than the
introduction of ArchUnit constraints alone. We do not use an
untreated control group, matching, or a difference-in-differences
design, and we do not estimate a causal treatment effect.
Moreover, the violation counts are right-skewed, with many
repositories reporting zero or relatively few violations and
some reporting substantially larger counts. Consequently,
the mean counts and OLS slopes may be influenced by repositories
with large violation counts. We interpret these estimates as
descriptive trends and consider them together with the reported
medians and interquartile ranges, rather than as evidence of a
uniform change across repositories.

\subsection{External Validity}
\label{sec:threats-external}

Our repository discovery relies on GitHub Search API queries
for artifacts associated with ArchUnit, import-linter, and
dependency-cruiser. The resulting corpus covers public
repositories indexed by GitHub at the time of the search.
Private, deleted, or unindexed repositories are not represented.
Our eligibility criteria further exclude forks, archived
repositories, templates and scaffolding projects, and
repositories with less than six months between creation and
their latest push. These criteria may underrepresent short-lived
projects and repositories that are no longer actively maintained.
Repositories with fewer than two contributors and repositories
without a release tag remain in the corpus when they meet the
other eligibility criteria.

Our findings therefore characterize the use and evolution of
the three selected tools in the observed public GitHub
repositories. They may not generalize to other architecture
conformance tools, programming ecosystems, proprietary projects,
or repositories hosted on other platforms. Similarly, our CI
analysis covers GitHub Actions workflows on the default branch.
Other CI systems, such as Jenkins and GitLab CI, and workflows
available only on other branches are outside the scope of our
observations.
