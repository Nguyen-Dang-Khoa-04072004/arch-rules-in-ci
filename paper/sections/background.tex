\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:background-related-work}

\subsection{Architectural Erosion and Executable Conformance Checking}

Software architecture captures the fundamental structures of a system, the relationships among its elements, and the rationale behind its design decisions \cite{bass2012,perry1992}. As software evolves, however, the implementation does not always remain aligned with these intended structures. Over time, deviations such as broken layering, cyclic dependencies, and unauthorized dependencies between components may accumulate, gradually leading to architectural erosion \cite{desilva2012,perry1992,passos2010}. Beyond compromising architectural consistency, these deviations can make a system increasingly difficult to maintain and evolve \cite{bass2012,passos2010}.

To address this challenge, architecture conformance checking examines whether an implementation continues to respect its intended architectural structures and constraints. A range of techniques, including dependency-structure matrices, source-code query languages, and reflexion models, have been developed to identify discrepancies between design and implementation \cite{passos2010}. More recently, executable architectural constraints have provided a way to make architectural decisions directly verifiable against source code or compiled artifacts. This approach resonates with the concept of architectural fitness functions, which use automated checks to assess whether important architectural characteristics are preserved throughout development \cite{ford2017}. Related model-based approaches further explore how design-time constraints can be translated into reusable executable checks \cite{kallel2017generating}.

Although integrating conformance checks into continuous integration (CI) brings architectural verification into the regular development workflow, allowing violations to be detected as code changes are integrated \cite{pinto2017}, defining architectural constraints is only part of ensuring conformance. Those constraints must also be checked consistently as the system changes. Moreover, the presence of CI checks does not necessarily mean that architectural constraints remain active or retain their original enforcement scope over time. Their continued effectiveness also depends on how they are maintained as the repository evolves.

\subsection{Constraint Enforcement, Maintenance, and Rule Rot}

The presence of executable architecture rules does not guarantee that they continue to prevent architectural violations. Developers may change rule configurations to tolerate existing violations, exclude parts of the system, or stop checks from failing a build. Such changes can weaken enforcement even when the rule files remain in the repository. Therefore, studying executable constraints requires looking at both how rules are defined and invoked and how their enforcement changes over time.

We identify two ways in which enforcement can be weakened: exemption and disabling. An exemption keeps a rule active but reduces the set of violations it can detect and report as failures. For example, ArchUnit's \texttt{FreezingArchRule} uses a baseline to tolerate existing violations while detecting new ones. In import-linter, \texttt{ignore\_imports} excludes selected import relationships. Similarly, dependency-cruiser supports path-based exceptions that exclude selected modules from a rule. These mechanisms preserve part of the rule's enforcement while allowing certain violations to be ignored.

Disabling, in contrast, prevents a rule from contributing to a failing check. This can happen when an ArchUnit rule is skipped using \texttt{@ArchIgnore}, when a rule is commented out or deleted, or when its configuration allows violations without failing the build. A disabled rule may no longer run, while a rule with a non-failing severity may still run and report violations. We therefore distinguish between rule execution and failure-producing enforcement. A rule can still execute and produce output without preventing architectural violations.

In this study, we use the term \emph{rule rot} to describe changes that weaken the failure-producing enforcement of executable architecture constraints through exemptions or disabling. Not every rule modification is rule rot. Changes that clarify a constraint, improve its implementation, or preserve its enforcement do not weaken the rule. Rule rot occurs when a change reduces the set of violations that can cause a check to fail.

%To study rule rot, we examine rule configurations together with CI workflows. Rule configurations show which constraints are defined and whether their enforcement has been narrowed or disabled. CI workflows provide evidence of whether the corresponding checkers are configured to run during development. For import-linter and dependency-cruiser, we identify commands that invoke the checkers. For ArchUnit, we examine build and test configurations that may execute architectural tests. The presence of a command or configuration indicates a potential or configured invocation, not proof that the check was successfully executed. Examining both rule configurations and CI workflows helps distinguish between defining architectural constraints and enforcing them.

\subsection{Architectural Conformance in Practice and Configuration Evolution}

The adoption of architectural conformance practices involves organizational and maintenance challenges beyond technical concerns. Although automated techniques can detect architectural violations, their practical value depends on whether developers incorporate them into everyday workflows and respond to the detected issues. Studies have shown that architecture consistency is often maintained through informal activities, highlighting that the availability of conformance techniques alone does not ensure their sustained use \cite{ali2018architecture}.

Integrating conformance checks into continuous integration (CI) can make architectural verification more continuous and actionable \cite{pinto2017}. However, adopting a tool, integrating it into a development workflow, and maintaining the constraints it evaluates are distinct activities. The presence of automated checks does not guarantee that violations will be corrected or that the checks will retain their original enforcement scope.

The maintenance of executable constraints also relates to broader configuration evolution. Denisov-Blanch et al. \cite{denisov2026ase} found that 73.8\% of AI-configuration artifacts across 441 corporate repositories were committed once and never modified during an approximately 14-month observation period. Although their study examines different artifact types, it illustrates how committed configuration files may receive little subsequent maintenance. This provides context for examining whether executable architecture rules remain unchanged or evolve in ways that weaken their enforcement.

\subsection{Research Gap and Study Positioning}

Our study addresses these gaps through a longitudinal examination
of executable architecture constraints in repositories using
ArchUnit, import-linter, and dependency-cruiser. We examine
architectural rule constructs to characterize the constraints
developers specify, inspect GitHub Actions workflows to assess
evidence of CI integration, and reconstruct rule histories to
identify how constraints are modified over time. We further
investigate changes that weaken enforcement, distinguishing
exemptions from disabling, and examine architectural violation
trajectories in the ArchUnit adoption cohort.

By connecting rule specification, CI integration, and rule
evolution, our study treats executable architecture governance
as more than the presence of automated checks. It examines
whether constraints are defined, whether there is evidence
that they are integrated into development workflows, and
whether their failure-producing enforcement is maintained
as repositories evolve. This perspective provides a basis
for understanding the prevalence and evolution of rule rot
alongside the broader adoption and maintenance of executable
architecture constraints.
