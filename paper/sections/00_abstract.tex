\begin{abstract}
Although executable checks are widely advocated as a means of enforcing architectural constraints, empirical evidence of their real-world adoption, the types of rules they encode, and their long-term evolution remains limited. To address this gap, we analyzed 4,300 GitHub repositories containing executable architecture-checking artifacts from ArchUnit, import-linter, and dependency-cruiser. We excluded forks, archived repositories, and templates, and required each repository to have a lifespan of at least six months between its creation and most recent push, with rules introduced between 2017 and 2026. We then normalized the rules using a canonical schema and reconstructed the histories of 11,098 rule files.
Our results reveal differences across tools in workflow integration, rule content, and rule evolution. Regarding workflow integration, 42.5\% of repositories using import-linter and 11.6\% of those using dependency-cruiser contained direct references to the respective tools in GitHub Actions workflows, while 35.9\% of repositories using ArchUnit integrated it into their build or test workflows. The tools also differed in the types of architectural constraints they enforced. Dependency and layering constraints accounted for 98.1\% of the rules in repositories using import-linter, compared with 58.5\% for ArchUnit and 56.8\% for dependency-cruiser. While cycle detection accounted for 43.2\% of dependency-cruiser rules, ArchUnit rules were more frequently focused on naming and annotation constraints.
We define rule rot as the exemption or disabling of architectural checks within a repository and find that its cumulative incidence over 24 months was 12.9\% for ArchUnit, 58.1\% for import-linter, and 80.5\% for dependency-cruiser. Meanwhile, 49.6\% of rule files remained unchanged throughout the observation period.
\end{abstract}
